# Extended contextual checks

These 65 checks are the earlier skill checklist, retained for a requested exhaustive audit. Use them as questions, not as an automatic rewrite rule or proof of authorship.

## Apply the 65 checks

### Vocabulary and inflated language

1. Review clusters of familiar model-associated vocabulary. Keep a word
   when it expresses the intended meaning precisely; do not blacklist it.

2. Remove exaggerated claims of importance unless the evidence supports
   that level of emphasis.

3. Explain actual complexity instead of merely calling a subject complex,
   nuanced, multifaceted, or intricate.

4. Replace decorative metaphors with direct descriptions when imagery
   does not help the reader understand.

5. Match vocabulary to the occasion. Keep ordinary correspondence
   readable and academic writing appropriately technical.

6. Reduce evaluative adjectives. Describe the achievement, property,
   consequence, or evidence behind the evaluation.

7. Prefer familiar, accurate words over unnecessarily elaborate choices.
   Retain distinctions that a simpler substitute would lose.

8. Preserve the author's recognizable vocabulary and level of expression.
   Do not turn every writer into the same polished narrator.

### Rhetorical habits

9. Reduce repeated corrective contrasts. State the intended claim
   directly unless correcting a real misunderstanding.

10. Avoid repeatedly framing comparisons as “less X, more Y.”
    Compare the relevant properties explicitly.

11. Remove counterarguments introduced only to give the paragraph
    something convenient to reject.

12. Review repeated groups of three. Let the material determine the
    number of examples, qualities, and supporting points.

13. Examine promises of benefits without costs. Identify material
    trade-offs when relevant and supported.

14. Replace announcements of importance with the actual consequence.
    Explain what changes, for whom, or under which conditions.

15. Use intensifiers sparingly. Let evidence carry the force of a claim.

16. Simplify excessively staged or mannered prose. Retain deliberate
    rhetorical effects when the genre and purpose justify them.

### Sentences and rhythm

17. Review repeated participial clauses and trailing “-ing” phrases.
    Keep them only when their relationship to the main clause is clear.

18. Turn unnecessary nominalizations into actions when that clarifies
    who did what. Preserve established technical terms.

19. Unpack dense noun phrases when readers must reconstruct the
    relationships between several abstract concepts.

20. Remove adjective stacks that add bulk without precision.

21. Vary repeated grammatical scaffolding when it makes the passage
    monotonous. Do not force variation into clear parallel instructions.

22. Let sentence length follow complexity and emphasis. Do not use
    numerical targets or mechanical short–long alternation.

23. Let paragraph boundaries follow changes in thought. Do not make
    paragraphs uniformly sized or artificially uneven.

24. Match syntax to genre. Allow conversation, reflection, instruction,
    and research reporting to use different forms.

### Organization

25. Replace generic introductions with the actual subject, situation,
    question, or finding.

26. Remove manufactured opening questions unless the question gives
    the reader a useful way into the topic.

27. Cut signposting that announces an obvious next step.

28. Keep transitions that express a real relationship. Remove linking
    phrases added merely to make prose sound formal.

29. Avoid imposing the same claim–explanation–summary pattern on every
    paragraph. Organize around the reasoning.

30. Make conclusions answer, qualify, or resolve the central question.
    Avoid repeating the introduction without development.

31. Remove automatic optimistic endings. State prospects only when
    there is a reason to expect them.

32. Allocate attention according to evidence and relevance. Do not
    manufacture equal weight between unequal positions.

### Substance and voice

33. Remove claims of sweeping significance that exceed the evidence.

34. Explain the reasoning between a fact and its interpretation.
    Do not attach an unsupported lesson to an observation.

35. Specify relationships described vaguely as connections,
    associations, influences, or contributions.

36. Name the relevant source or group when attributing an opinion.
    Avoid anonymous authority when attribution matters.

37. Preserve the scope of agreement. Do not turn one author's view
    into a consensus or a limited finding into a universal conclusion.

38. Include the practical details needed to understand the claim:
    setting, sample, procedure, mechanism, or example, as applicable.
    Use only details that are supplied or verified.

39. Match certainty to evidence. Preserve justified uncertainty and
    remove qualifications that serve no clear purpose.

40. Smooth accidental changes in voice across sections while preserving
    legitimate differences in function, authorship, or quotation.

### References and factual reliability

41. Check that cited publications exist when verification is needed.
    Do not construct plausible references from memory.

42. Check that authors, titles, journals, and other bibliographic
    elements belong to the same work.

43. Verify that identifiers resolve to the intended material when tools
    permit. Never invent a DOI, ISBN, or other identifier.

44. Check publication dates, volumes, issues, pages, and publishers
    against the original source when available.

45. Check whether each source supports the associated claim.
    Narrow or qualify the claim when the source supports less.

46. Check chronological consistency between events and references.

47. Check suspicious links. Distinguish an invented address from an
    ordinary typo, access restriction, redirect, or decayed link.

### Formatting and punctuation

48. Use bold to identify priorities, not to decorate every paragraph.

49. Use lists for genuinely parallel items, options, or steps.
    Keep connected reasoning in connected prose when clearer.

50. Use headings when they improve navigation. Avoid fragmenting
    short writing into unnecessary sections.

51. Make headings describe their content. Avoid forced symmetry,
    repeated paired nouns, or elaborate heading formulas.

52. Use dashes when they serve the sentence. Revise repetitive
    interruptions without banning a legitimate punctuation mark.

53. Follow the requested or established comma convention consistently.
    Do not treat the Oxford comma as an authorship signal.

54. Preserve the requested or established regional English conventions.
    Do not automatically Americanize or Anglicize the user's voice.

### Drafting and chatbot artifacts

55. Remove assistant introductions from the deliverable itself unless
    the requested format calls for them.

56. Remove assistant follow-up offers from finished documents.

57. Remove irrelevant model self-reference or training disclaimers from
    the deliverable. Preserve required disclosure and transparency.

58. Resolve placeholders using supplied or verified information.
    Never fill missing facts by guessing. Flag essential gaps briefly.

59. Render citations correctly and remove broken internal citation
    tokens. Preserve valid citations and source provenance.

60. Use formatting compatible with the destination. Remove accidental
    wrappers, escaped markup, or code fences around ordinary prose.
    Preserve code fences when the content is actually code.

### Wikipedia and institutional editing contexts

61. Replace self-justifying claims about media attention with relevant,
    neutrally stated facts and appropriate attribution.

62. Describe actual awards or achievements. Do not substitute vague
    recognition or prestige for substance.

63. Keep editing notes proportionate to the change. Explain decisions
    that help a reviewer assess the work.

64. Address the specific concern rather than repeatedly asserting good
    intentions, quality, neutrality, or compliance.

65. Verify policy references and their applicability before relying on
    them. Do not invent rules, shortcuts, or institutional requirements.
